## Context

- **F01.** About half of the materials extracted worldwide each year go into building up or renewing stocks: buildings, infrastructure and machinery. World, 2010. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F02.** Recycling supplies only 12% of the material flowing into those stocks. World, 2010. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Quote: "recycling still only contributes 12% of inflows to stocks." Confirmed.
- **F03.** Global material stocks grew 23-fold from 1900 to 2010, to 792 billion tonnes (plus or minus 5%). Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F04.** The authors' remedy is more service from existing stocks: use them more intensively, make them last longer and design them more efficiently. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F05.** The EU's circular material use rate rose from about 11.3% in 2015 to 11.7% in 2021. ECA 2023, paragraphs 25 and 93. Confirmed. Read in the French version, on Eurostat data as published in 2023.
- **F06.** Seven member states' rates fell over the same period. ECA 2023, paragraph 26. Confirmed. The report text names only Poland; news coverage lists Lithuania, Sweden, Romania, Denmark, Luxembourg, Finland and Poland.
- **F07.** The EU planned more than €10 billion for the circular economy in 2014 to 2020, and about three quarters of the €7.1 billion cohesion policy share was tied to EU waste law. ECA 2023, paragraphs IX, 55 and 59. Confirmed. Not "spent," and not 2016 to 2020 as some news coverage says.
- **F08.** Low circularity traces to energy use, 44% of processed materials, and to stocks that keep growing, with net additions of 17 Gt a year. World, 2005. The EU's circularity is low for similar reasons, despite its considerably higher end-of-life recycling rates. Haas et al. 2015, abstract. Seen in summary; the 17 Gt and the EU sentence confirmed against the abstract, through search results.
- **F09.** The EU28's input cycling rate was 9.6% in 2014, and the authors want mass indicators split by material to judge impact. Eurostat's Sankey descends from this framework. Mayer et al. 2019, abstract. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The paper doesn't say that longer lifetimes or reuse lower cycling indicators; it notes that in-use stocks delay output flows and that growing stocks inhibit loop closing (Methods).

## Main story

- **F10.** New material used is about eight times the recycled material used, so the rate follows material use more than recycling. EU-27, around 2021. EEA 2023, under "Main factors influencing the circular material use rate." Quote: "influenced more by trends in material consumption than by trends in recycling." Confirmed.
- **F11.** Recycling 70% of treated waste (40% in 2020), using 15% less material than in 2020 and a third less fossil fuel (34% against the baseline) together lift the rate to about 22% by 2030, short of 23.4%. No lever reaches the target alone, and the briefing gives no rate for each. EEA 2023, under "Prospects for the EU reaching its target" and in the key messages. Confirmed. Its key messages: "Enhanced waste recycling alone will not be sufficient for the EU to achieve the target," and, under the prospects section, "none of the scenarios alone would achieve the target of doubling the CMUR by 2030"; recycling 90% of all waste "could enable the EU to almost meet the target by 2050." Through an automated fetch (November 23). Backs the opening's "the EEA has warned since 2023 that recycling won't get there." It doesn't say how far any one lever would have to go.
- **F12.** Doubling from 11.7% in 2020 to 23.4% in 2030 means the rate climbing by 1.3% of material used each year, against 0.1% a year over "the past decade," which the briefing doesn't define; it gives 0.4% a year for 2004 to 2010. The EEA's 23.4% doubles the 2020 rate; the page's 24% is the Clean Industrial Deal's (F34). EEA 2023, same section. Confirmed.
- **F13.** The world economy was more circular in 1900 than in 2015: the share of material inputs that cycled, biomass included, fell from 43% (41 to 51%) to 27% (25 to 30%), while non-circular inputs grew 16-fold. Biomass's ecological cycling made up 91% of cycling in 1900 and 76% in 2015. Haas et al. 2020, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F14.** The authors rank absolute cuts in non-circular flows above maximizing recycling rates, one of four challenges they name with curbing stock growth, criteria for ecological cycling and decarbonizing energy. Haas et al. 2020, abstract. Quote: "prioritizing absolute reductions of non-circular flows over maximizing (re)cycling rates." Confirmed.
- **F15.** A review of 835 studies found few lasting cases of growth with falling resource use; the authors call for sufficiency and enforced absolute reduction targets. Haberl et al. 2020. Seen in summary.
- **F16.** A recycled tonne doesn't automatically cancel a new one; the paper calls this circular economy rebound. Zink and Geyer 2017. Seen in summary.
- **F34.** The EU aims for 24% of its materials to be circular by 2030. Clean Industrial Deal, Commission page, under "Circularity and access to materials." Quote: "The aim is to have 24% of materials circular by 2030." Confirmed. Worded as an aim; the same section commits to adopting a circular economy act in 2026.
- **F49.** The EEA's indicator page gives the doubling as 22.4% by 2030 and the rate as 12.2% in 2024. The repo's 2020 rate is 11.2%, so 22.4% doubles the revised 2020 figure, where the EEA's 2023 briefing doubled 11.7% to 23.4% (F12) and the Deal says 24% (F34). EEA indicator, "Circular material use rate in Europe." Confirmed, through search results.
- **F60.** The EEA's 2025 assessment says the rate rose from 10.7% in 2010 to 12.2% in 2024, the rise to 2022 "largely driven by increased waste recycling" and the rise from 2022 to 2024 by lower domestic material consumption, "mainly due to decreased use of metals." By weight that doesn't hold in Eurostat's DMC, where the 2022 to 2024 fall was 56.9% fossil fuels and 42.9% minerals (`headline_numbers_2022.csv`): don't cite this attribution. It names reducing material consumption, through reuse and longer product lifetimes, beside boosting recycling as ways to reach the target, and judges the target "very unlikely." EEA, Monitoring report on progress towards the 8th EAP objectives 2025, December 2025, section 6.3. Confirmed.
- **F68.** No official text says which year's rate the Deal's 24% doubles. The Deal's communication says "from 11.8% today to 24% by 2030" (F38), 11.8% being 2023's rate. The Commission's circular economy page puts today's rate at "about 12%" and the goal as doubling it to 24% (DG ENV, through search results). COM(2025) 805, of December 23, 2025, speaks of the "ambition to double Europe's circularity rate to 24% by 2030," citing 2024's 12.2% (through an automated fetch). The doubling from 2020 belongs to the 8th Environment Action Programme, whose monitoring target is to double the rate "by 2030 compared to 2020" (EEA 8th EAP scoreboard 2025, through search results), and the EEA's indicator says the Deal confirmed that ambition while "slightly increasing the target to 24%" over its 22.4% (EEA indicator, published November 28, 2025, through search results). The 2020 plan sets no year (F37). So the Method note's "each doubles a different starting point" holds for the EEA's two figures only: the Deal's 24% is the Commission's doubling of a rate of about 12%, and the EEA reads it as above a doubling of 2020.
- **F98.** The newest published EU rate is 12.2%, for 2024, the highest recorded, up from 10.7% in 2010 (Eurostat news, November 19, 2025, through search results; env_ac_cur as downloaded, `headline_numbers_2022.csv`, `rate_2024`, `rate_2010`, `rise_2010_2024`). env_ac_cur flags every 2024 rate, the EU's included, as imputed; 2024's waste treatment (env_wastrt) was first published October 28, 2026, after the rate, which hasn't been recalculated since. The page calls 2024's rate Eurostat's estimate. No 2025 rate was found as of November 23, 2026. Reaching 24% by 2030 from 2024's rate takes a rise of about 2% of material used a year (`pace_needed_per_year_from_2024`), more than the whole rise from 2010 to 2024 (from 10.7% to 12.2%). The Commission gives the same two ends: "from 2010 to 2024, the share of recycled materials in the economy increased only slightly from 10.7% to 12.2%" (COM(2025) 805, p. 1; through search results).

## Recycling replacing new material

The page assumes each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, a midpoint between replacing none and replacing a whole tonne (brief, The piece). These findings back the two bounds and the limits of replacement.

- **F54.** ETC CE 2025/3 models enhanced recycling with 1:1 substitution: "we assume a 1:1 substitution rate in which 1 kg of waste sent to recycling replaces 1 kg of primary materials"; footnote 34: "this is likely an overestimation"; and "this scenario should be considered as an upper limit of the potential of enhanced recycling." Primary production falls through reduced demand, so consumption isn't held stable. ETC CE 2025/3, section 4.3 (results in 5.3). Confirmed, through an automated fetch.
- **F55.** Eurostat reads a higher rate as more secondary materials substituting for primary raw materials, avoiding the impacts of extracting them. Eurostat, metadata for the circularity rate (env_ac_cur), section 3.1. Confirmed, through search results. The indicator's own framing assumes substitution; it doesn't measure it.
- **F56.** Displacement is set by markets, not physics, and is not guaranteed. For US aluminum, 100% displacement is unlikely right after a lasting rise in recycling and even less likely in the long term; zero and even negative displacement are possible, and the estimates are sensitive to price elasticities. Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018, abstract. Confirmed, through search results. One material, one country: no economy-wide rate. The authors' 2016 paper notes that assessments have tended to assume one-to-one displacement because no consensus method exists. No sourced middle-ground rate (such as 50%) has been found for the EU's materials.
- **F57.** In 2022, 40.8% of EU waste was recycled and 30.2% landfilled, and 64% of all waste generated was major mineral waste, from mining, quarrying, construction and demolition. Eurostat, Statistics Explained, "Waste statistics." Confirmed, through search results (the 64% source split from the European Parliament's summary of the same data). The 40.8% matches the rate's 41% of treated waste recycled. The rate's recycled waste is recycling alone, R2 to R11, energy recovery and backfilling excluded, from env_wastrt, net of waste traded for recycling (env_ac_cur metadata, sections 3.1, 3.4 and 18.1; confirmed, through an automated fetch); rebuilt that way in the repo, 41.1% of treated waste was recycled in 2022. The waste not yet recycled is mostly minerals, checked November 20 in the repo (`waste_by_material_2022.csv`, `headline_numbers_2022.csv`): 86.5% by material (env_wassd), and 75.8% major mineral waste by category, about 82% with ash and mineral residues (env_wastrt).
- **F59.** In a model of the copper market, permanent increases in recycling displace about 0.5 kt of mine production per kt of extra scrap supply on average, short-lived recycling policies can raise mine production, and displacement is lower for large changes in scrap supply. Ryter et al. 2022, abstract. Confirmed, through search results. One metal: scale only, never the basis for the page's midpoint.
- **F61.** Recycling takes new material and energy of its own. Keeping materials in perfect condition as they cycle, "without input of new materials or energy," would defy entropy; a fully circular economy would need the second law of thermodynamics annulled. Cullen 2018, MRS Bulletin editorial. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. In Cullen 2017, every pass through a loop dissipates material, losing quantity (material lost, or turned into by-products) and quality (mixing, downgrading), and a fully circular economy is a theoretical benchmark to measure progress against, like a perpetual motion machine if taken as a goal (refficiency.org's introduction to the paper; through an automated fetch). His Circularity Index multiplies a quantity ratio (recovered end-of-life material over total demand) by a quality ratio (the energy recovery saves against primary production): concrete 0.00, paper 0.04, plastic 0.07, steel 0.14 and aluminum 0.20, where 1 is perfect and thermodynamically impossible; recovering aluminum takes about 4% of primary production's energy, yet recovered metal meets about 21% of world demand (Discard Studies, November 13, 2017, through an automated fetch; the index's two ratios from a summary Milcah supplied, November 22). The paper itself wasn't opened. World. A principle for the page, not EU figures.
- **F62.** Recycling loses part of what it collects. Primary data from EU plants handling plastic packaging give median yields at sorting plants of 85% for PET and HDPE, 64% for PP, 59% for films and 37% for PS, and at recycling plants of 81% (PET), 88% (HDPE), 66% (PP), 71% (films) and 66% (PS). Of the plastic packaging waste the EU27 generated in 2017, about 14% became recyclate in the EU, 25% counting exports as recycled. Some recyclate goes into lower-grade products, such as coat hangers and benches. Antonopoulos, Faraca and Tonini 2021 (JRC), abstract, section 3.1, Table 2 (medians; the text's range for films differs from the table), sections 3.3 and 3.4. Quote: "significant losses of target materials occurred both at sorting and recycling stages." Confirmed, through an automated fetch. Plastics only; the paper doesn't say recyclate is blended with virgin polymer, or give the plants' energy use.
- **F63.** Some loops need new material added. Paper: Europe's paper industry says fibers can be recycled many times while the loop is "replenished with virgin fibres," and that packaging fibers were used 5.5 times on average in 2019 (Cepi, position paper summary, September 20, 2022; confirmed, through search results; an industry source). Aluminum: aluminum has few ways to be refined once melted, so the industry cascades scrap into casting alloys or dilutes it with primary metal; in one assessment, engine and transmission scrap could supply only 7.8% of a can-body alloy (AA3104) but 99.7% of a die-casting alloy, and used cans need no dilution (Paraskevas, Kellens, Renaldi, Dewulf and Duflou 2013, conference paper, abstract and section 5; through an automated fetch). Concrete: concrete with much recycled aggregate usually holds more cement than concrete with natural aggregate, because recycled aggregate demands more water (JRC 2023, introduction p. 4 and section 3.3.1 p. 21; confirmed, through an automated fetch). Concrete overlaps task 6a: cite this entry for the extra cement only, not for caps on recycled content.
- **F64.** Recycling's energy is mostly far below primary production's, so it trims the climate credit rather than cancelling it. Secondary aluminum took 8.3 GJ of primary energy per tonne in 2019 against 186 GJ for primary aluminum, a 95.5% saving (International Aluminium Institute; an industry source, through search results). Steel from scrap in electric arc furnaces typically uses 60 to 70% less energy (IPCC AR4, Working Group III, 2007, section 7.3.6; through search results). Producing recycled aggregate typically takes less energy than natural aggregate, one study giving 15.5 kg of CO2 per tonne against 103 kg (JRC 2023, section 3.3.2, pp. 23 to 25; through an automated fetch). But swapping half or all of concrete's natural coarse aggregate for recycled aggregate can raise its emissions slightly when transport is left out (by 0.3% for normal strength and 3.4% for high strength at half), since cement causes most of concrete's emissions, 74 to 81% in typical mixes by an earlier study they cite (Sabău, Bompa and Silva 2021, abstract and section 1; through an automated fetch; section 3.1 gives 0.7% for normal strength). Recycling's energy belongs in the climate caveat (panel 4's 3.6% "is generous"), not in the second hope: it doesn't change how many new tonnes a recycled one replaces.
- **F71.** Concrete's European standard recommends caps on recycled aggregate but doesn't set them. EN 206 always allows up to 5% of natural aggregate to be replaced by reclaimed aggregate from a producer's own concrete; for recycled aggregate from other sources it only gives recommendations (Helsing, Brander and Martinsson 2024, section 1.3; through search results). Annex E, Table E.2 recommends at most, by mass of the coarse aggregate (4 mm and over): for Type A (at least 90% concrete, and 95% concrete and unbound stone together: Rc90 and Rcu95 under EN 12620), 50% in X0 (no risk of corrosion or attack), 30% in XC1 to XC4, XF1, XA1 and XD1, and 0% in all other exposure classes; for Type B (Rc50, Rcu70), 50% in X0, 20% in XC1 and XC2, and 0% elsewhere, with no Type B above strength class C30/37. Type A from a known source may go up to 30% in the classes its original concrete was designed for. Coarse aggregate only: the table gives no share for fine recycled aggregate. EN 206:2013+A2:2021, Annex E, Table E.2, as reproduced in Nomikou et al. 2023, Table 1 (through an automated fetch). The standard itself, sold by CEN members, wasn't opened, so partly sourced: cite it on the page only as "European and national rules", never with Table E.2's numbers.
- **F72.** National rules set the binding caps, and they differ. Germany: under the DAfStb guideline (2010), now taken into DIN 1045-2:2023-08, recycled aggregate may make up at most 45% (Type 1, mostly concrete) or 35% (Type 2, mixed rubble) by volume of all aggregate, depending on exposure class, and only up to strength class C30/37 (Baunetz Wissen; through search results); it is excluded for chlorides (XD, XS) and wear (XM) (GRBV symposium slides, 2024; through search results); under DIN 1045-2:2023, up to 25% by volume of the coarse aggregate counts as normal concrete (Breit and Burgmann 2023, abstract; IFEU slides, 2022; through search results). The JRC's table of national maximums gives, among others: Spain 20% concrete-waste aggregate without design changes, up to C40/50; the Netherlands 50% high-quality and 20% moderate-quality aggregate, low to moderate exposure, up to C35/45; France 30% coarse high-quality concrete-waste aggregate in low-demanding classes, less for lower grades; Belgium 30% in reinforced concrete up to C30/37 and 50% in unreinforced. JRC 2023, section 4, Table 9, "Maximum allowed incorporation ratios of RA for structural concrete," p. 38 (through an automated fetch). Some Member States are left out for inconsistent sources; the fetch read Germany's chloride cell as 35%, against the German sources: don't cite that cell. Partly sourced.
- **F73.** The caps aren't what keeps rubble out of concrete. The JRC finds that "current regulations are not a limiting factor" for the uptake of recycled aggregate concrete (section 4, p. 39); in its survey of aggregate producers, the share of recycled aggregate sold for concrete "varied between 0 and 3%" (Austria and Belgium aside, their figures unclear), the rest going to unknown purposes or to backfilling and road construction (section 6, p. 50); recycled aggregates are "downcycled and rarely used in concrete," and if a 30% mean incorporation were reached, about 30% of all non-soil construction and demolition waste generated each year would be recovered (repository summary). JRC 2023: sections 4 and 6 through an automated fetch of characters 100,000 to 200,000; summary confirmed, through search results. A Swedish review agrees: where rules and standards permit it, the amount used in concrete is "negligible" in many countries (Helsing, Brander and Martinsson 2024, abstract; through search results). No EU-wide split by use was found. So concrete bears on the second hope twice: rules let it take only part of its stone from rubble, and in practice it takes almost none. Builds on: by the JRC's 30% scenario, concrete taking 30% recycled aggregate everywhere would use only about a third of non-soil construction and demolition waste, so most rubble would still go under roads or into fill (our reading).
- **F74.** Rubble is already mostly recycled; what is left to recycle is mostly mine rock and soil. In env_wastrt, the EU treated 282.2 Mt of mineral waste from construction and demolition (W121) in 2022 and recycled 224.9 Mt of it (79.7%), backfilled 30.5 Mt (10.8%, not counted in the rate) and landfilled 25.2 Mt; it was 27.8% of all EU recycling. Of the 1,161.8 Mt of treated waste not recycled, other mineral wastes (W12B) were 40.7% (473.1 Mt; 12.5% of them recycled), soils (W126) 25.8% (299.4 Mt; 35.4% recycled, 202.1 Mt backfilled), construction and demolition mineral waste 4.9% and combustion wastes (W124) 4.9%. In env_wasgen, mining and quarrying generated 87.6% of the EU's W12B in 2022 (489.1 of 558.6 Mt). Treatment before trade is netted; the totals give F57's 41.1% recycled. Our calculation, November 22. So caps on recycled aggregate in concrete bear on rubble, which is under 5% of what is left; panel 4's route to 93% mostly means recycling mine rock and soil, which have few uses beyond fill and earthworks (our judgment; Estonia's hill, F69, is one case). Supports both hopes: reach (what is left is the hardest waste to use) and replacement (what it would replace is the cheapest stone, if anything). Where it goes (November 23, `headline_numbers_2022.csv`, `not_recycled_*`): about three quarters of the waste not recycled is buried, landfilled (D1, D5, D12) or backfilled, and the rest goes to other disposal (D2 to D4, D6, D7, which includes surface impoundment) or is burned. So "buried" holds for most of it, not all; the page gives no share.
- **F75.** Steel has no set share of scrap; its limit is quality. Copper over 0.1% by weight causes hot shortness, surface cracking in hot rolling and forming, and tin worsens it even at 0.04%; copper cannot be removed commercially once in the melt, so dilution with virgin iron or cleaner scrap is the only commercially practiced remedy, besides trading contaminated scrap to tolerant uses such as reinforcing bar. Daehn, Cabrera Serrenho and Allwood 2017, abstract and introduction (through search results of the open-access text). Their abstract says copper from conventional scrap preparation can be managed globally until 2050; Daehn's thesis says that if current scrap preparation continues, more copper will enter the steel cycle than demanded products can tolerate by 2050, and sooner if primary production is cut (Daehn 2019, abstract; through search results): world, a projection, not EU figures. In the EU, flat steel "today comes almost exclusively from primary steel production," in which no post-consumer scrap is used; flat products generally tolerate less than 0.06% copper; flat steel is the largest share of steel in a passenger car; with appropriate processing, 30% recycled content from closed-loop recycling in car manufacturing is feasible; the EU produced more than 126 Mt of steel in 2023. UBA, AutoRess Factsheet 10, November 19, 2025, pp. 1 to 3 (through an automated fetch). Sourced. Supports the second hope: a scrap tonne replaces a new one only in products that tolerate its copper, and elsewhere needs new iron added (as with aluminum, F63). By weight it barely moves the rate (metal ores are 5.5% of the disc), but metals are 16.3% of the climate impact of making materials (F17) and about half imported (F39).
- **F76.** Other limits checked, none high-volume enough for the page. Asphalt: the EU-27 made 212.5 Mt of hot and warm mix asphalt in 2022 and 202.7 Mt in 2023 (EAPA, Asphalt in Figures 2023, Table 1; through an automated fetch); of 36 Mt of reclaimed asphalt available in the reporting countries in 2022, 76% was re-used in new asphalt, 22% recycled in other uses and 2% landfilled or unknown (World Highways, February 20, 2024, reporting EAPA; through search results); Germany re-used 90% in new asphalt in 2023 (Table 5). Limits on reclaimed asphalt in road specifications weren't found; with most already going back into asphalt, what binds is how much comes back (the first hope), not a cap. Food-contact plastic: under Regulation (EU) 2022/1616, recycled plastic may touch food only if made by a suitable technology, and only two are established, mechanical PET recycling and recycling in a closed and controlled product loop (Commission food safety page; through search results); the EU generated 16.16 Mt of plastic packaging waste in 2022 and recycled 6.58 Mt (European Parliament, through search results), about 0.25% of the 6.4 billion tonnes of new material consumed (our arithmetic). Container glass: 12.4 Mt collected in 2022, 80.2% (Close the Glass Loop, an industry source; through search results), about 0.2%; color limits not checked. Medical and pharmaceutical packaging and textiles: not checked, lighter still. A cap on a light material barely moves a rate measured by weight.

## Holding recycling fixed

Still the rule for cuts: when the reader or a scenario stops using new material, waste and recycling stay at 2022's tonnes.

- **F46.** The EEA's scenarios for the doubling target cut material consumption while keeping circular use constant, and balance the mass through less stock accumulation and fewer emissions, not less waste (section 4.2, Table 2). The 70% recycling scenario holds consumption, waste traded and waste generated stable (section 4.3.1), so in it recycled tonnes add to new ones; the page's midpoint doesn't follow that part (F54). The EEA's chart of the scenarios calls them a first indicative estimate, without a full mass-balancing exercise. The report also says that using less fossil fuel and biomass for energy, and preventing food waste, lower the volumes available for recycling (section 2.3.2, p. 15); that biomass and fossil fuels largely become emissions, while metals and minerals go into stock and become available for recycling later (section 2.3.3, pp. 17 to 18); and that long-lasting products reach recycling after a delay (section 4.1). Its combined 2050 scenario (90% recycling, 45% less material and 83% less fossil fuel) reaches 39% (key message 4.4, p. 25). ETC CE Report 2023/6. Quote: "while keeping U constant." Confirmed, through an automated fetch of the PDF's first 100,000 of 169,506 characters, to section 4.3.1.
- **F47.** The EEA's country briefings record rates running high when consumption fell but recycling didn't. In Belgium, recycling fell less than material consumption, so the rate was exceptionally high in those years; in Estonia, the rate peaked in 2012, when consumption was lower but recycling about the same. EEA, Europe's environment 2025, country briefings on the circular material use rate. Confirmed, through search results.
- **F48.** Dynamic stock-flow models trace how material inputs build in-use stocks and come back as end-of-life waste over their lifetimes: MISO, an inflow-driven extension of economy-wide material flow accounting (Wiedenhofer et al. 2019), and MISO2, a country-level version covering 14 processes from extraction to recycling and waste management, and 13 end-uses of stocks (Wiedenhofer et al. 2024). The route to modeling how waste follows material use over time; not used on the page. Seen in summary.
- **F58.** Growing stocks keep material loops open: when inflows to stocks are a multiple of outflows, closing loops is hard even with better end-of-life recycling, and if net additions keep declining, stocks in the industrial world may saturate, which "would increase the potential for closed loops and absolute reductions in primary material extraction." The authors expect 35% of the 2010 stock to be discarded by 2030. World. Krausmann et al. 2017, discussion. Confirmed, through an automated fetch of the full text. For the United States, "Stabilized stocks also result in stable EoL outflows after 2050, whereas ongoing stock growth makes outflows grow until 2080." Streeck et al. 2021, abstract. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. Neither models waste falling with a cut in use: that the link between use and waste tightens as stocks stop growing is our reading.
- **F47, checked November 22.** The Estonia example doesn't hold in Eurostat's current data: from 2010 to 2012 Estonia's circular use rose 162.6% while its consumption rose 9.3% (`trend_steps.csv`), so its 2012 peak came from recycling (F69). Belgium's doesn't stand out either: its only two-year step since 2010 in which both fell, 2014 to 2016, took its rate from 17.9% to 18.0%. The briefings likely used older data. Don't cite F47's examples on the page.

## The economic case

- **F37.** The doubling goal dates from the 2020 Circular Economy Action Plan, which set it in the same sentence as cutting the consumption footprint, under the subtitle "For a cleaner and more competitive Europe." The plan says EU manufacturing firms spend on average about 40% on materials, and cites a study estimating an extra 0.5% of GDP and around 700,000 jobs by 2030 (Cambridge Econometrics, Trinomics and ICF 2018; not used, see the brief's Flags). COM(2020) 98, Introduction. Quote: "strive to reduce its consumption footprint and double its circular material use rate in the coming decade." Confirmed, through an automated fetch of a mirror copy; cite EUR-Lex on the page.
- **F38.** The Clean Industrial Deal's communication makes the rate a KPI: "Increase circular material use rate from 11.8% today to 24% by 2030," with footnote 28 citing the 2020 plan (F37) and footnote 29 saying progress is reported in the Annual Single Market and Competitiveness Report. Its flagship table lists the Circular Economy Act for Q4 2026. It gives circularity's reasons as maximizing limited resources, reducing dependencies, resilience, lower production costs and lower CO2, and projects the remanufacturing market growing from €31 billion to €100 billion by 2030, creating 500,000 jobs (not used). It never mentions material consumption, sufficiency or using less: "footprint" appears once, for labels showing consumers the carbon footprint of products (section 3), "consumption" only for energy, and "sufficient" only in its everyday sense. COM(2025) 85, sections 1, 3 and 5 and the flagship actions table. Confirmed: searched by hand in the EUR-Lex PDF by Milcah and by script on November 21.
- **F39.** The EU is almost self-sufficient in non-metallic minerals and biomass and highly dependent on imported metal ores and fossil fuels. Import dependency, decade averages: minerals 3%, biomass 11%, metal ores 51%, fossil fuels 70% (env_ac_mid; data from June 2026). Eurostat, "Physical imports and exports," Table 1. Quote: "The EU economy is almost self-sufficient in the supply of non-metallic minerals (notably construction materials) and biomass." Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The page's 2022 values come from the repo (brief, "Numbers behind it").
- **F40.** The Critical Raw Materials Act sets 2030 benchmarks for 17 strategic raw materials (Annex I): EU capacity able to mine at least 10% of the EU's yearly consumption of them, process at least 40% and recycle at least 25%, and no more than 65% of yearly consumption of any one of them from a single third country. They are benchmarks for capacity that the EU should "approach or reach," not shares of actual supply. It also asks the Commission and member states to "moderate the expected increase" in the EU's consumption of critical raw materials through technological progress and resource efficiency. And by January 1, 2027, the Commission must set recycling benchmarks per strategic raw material, as a share of what is available in relevant waste streams. In force since May 23, 2024. Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, Article 5, Annex I. Confirmed.
- **F41.** Of the 26 critical raw materials the EU needs for the energy transition, 10 are not recycled at all (lithium, gallium and silicon metal among them) and 7 have end-of-life recycling input rates of 1 to 5%. The ECA also finds that the potential to cut the EU's need for primary critical raw materials through circularity, substitution and resource efficiency is "not fully used." ECA Special Report 04/2026, adopted December 10, 2025, paragraphs 18, 19 and 83 and Figure 19. Confirmed.
- **F42.** Commissioner Roswall's list for the Circular Economy Act on November 13, 2026: one extended producer responsibility registration through a single representative, harmonized and faster end-of-waste criteria, more demand for recycled materials and circular products, stronger e-waste collection to recover critical raw materials, and transregional circularity hubs. No material use target or 24% rate mentioned. The article says the Act is "expected in 2027" without saying whether that means the proposal. Agence Europe, November 13, 2026. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The Regulatory Scrutiny Board's main criticism, by Roswall's account, was that the demand side wasn't addressed enough (EUWID, October 2026; seen in summary).
- **F43.** A European Parliament briefing on the Act records 4.3 million people working in the EU's circular economy (2021), a material footprint of 14.1 tonnes per person (2024) far beyond sustainable levels, the Council's call for EU reduction targets, and the EEA's view that absolute cuts in resource use are needed. It repeats the 0.5% of GDP and 700,000 jobs without a source. EPRS briefing PE 782.628, Camille Siefridt, January 2026. Confirmed, through an automated fetch.
- **F44.** EU gas demand fell 18% between August 2022 and December 2023 (about 101 bcm), against a voluntary 15% target under Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1369. Commission review COM(2024) 88, in Council document 6566/24. Seen in summary.
- **F45.** The EEA's material footprint indicator says fossil fuels are the only material group whose footprint fell from 2010 to 2024, by 36%, which "could be attributed to EU climate policies, and other recent energy consumption-related developments, like high energy prices due to Russia's aggression in Ukraine." It puts the footprint's 6.2% fall in 2023 down mainly to metals and fossil fuels, metal consumption falling 16% that year as the EU imported fewer metals from Russia and exported more to Ukraine. The printed monitoring report (December 2025, section 3.1) gives only the war: lower use of fossil fuels and metals "resulting from the geopolitical response to the war in Ukraine." Backs panel 11's judgment, that years of decisions lie behind the fossil fall, as the EEA's hedged reading on a different measure.

## Weight versus climate

- **F17.** Non-metallic minerals are 52.3% of EU material use by weight but 11.4% of the climate impact of producing materials. EU-27, 2022, extraction to factory gate. ETC CE 2025/3, Figure 3-4 (about page 12), red climate series. Confirmed against the figure. All four groups' values are in `build_data.py` (WEIGHT_VS_CLIMATE); metal ores are 16.3% of climate impact, and their 30.2% is particulate matter.
- **F18.** Biomass is 22.7% of the weight and 99.5% of land-use biodiversity loss. ETC CE 2025/3, Figure 3-4; the text rounds it to 99%, on IRP GRO 2024 data. Confirmed.
- **F19.** The impacts cover making each material ready to use, up to the factory gate; burning fuel and later uses aren't allocated to the materials. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4 and the box in section 4.4. Confirmed. The chart needs a label saying so, or the fossil fuel share looks too small.
- **F96.** For oil and gas, burning them is most of their climate cost. Worldwide, the production, transport and processing of oil and gas (scope 1 and 2) caused 5.1 Gt CO2-eq in 2022, "just under 15%" of energy-related greenhouse gas emissions; "The use of the oil and gas results in another 40% of emissions." IEA 2023, Emissions from Oil and Gas Operations in Net Zero Transitions, report page, https://www.iea.org/reports/emissions-from-oil-and-gas-operations-in-net-zero-transitions. Confirmed, through an automated fetch (November 23). World, not EU, and oil and gas only: it doesn't cover coal. Backs the game's fossil fuels note and the game's note.
- **F20.** A higher circularity rate doesn't necessarily mean lower environmental impact. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4 and key messages (page 2). Quote: "does not necessarily lead to lower environmental impacts." Confirmed.
- **F21.** Making ready-to-use materials accounts for around 80% of the EU's total environmental impacts, seen as a footprint; the rest comes from the rest of the economy and households' direct impacts. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4. Confirmed. Measured as the environmental footprint score, not climate alone.
- **F22.** An older EEA figure gave non-metallic minerals 6% of the environmental footprint of ready-to-use materials. EU-27, 2019. EEA 2023, Figure 4. Confirmed. A composite score on a different method: never set it beside the 11.4%.
- **F23.** Impact per kilogram spans orders of magnitude, and bulk materials rank low. van der Voet et al. 2004. Seen in summary.
- **F24.** Biomass drives more than 90% of land-related biodiversity loss and water stress. IRP Global Resources Outlook 2024. Seen in summary. Not the IRP's older line that all resource extraction and processing cause over 90%.
- **F25.** Producing materials caused 23% of global emissions in 2015, about two-fifths of it for construction. Hertwich 2021. Seen in summary. The IRP 2020 report page gives the same 23% for 2015, up from 15% in 1995.
- **F53.** The climate change impact of the raw materials used in the EU-27 was 2.89E+12 kg CO2-eq in 2022 (3.09E+12 in 2010, 2.74E+12 in 2021), so 2.89 billion tonnes, the total behind the game's carbon tonnes. A cradle-to-gate footprint of EU final demand, on the Environmental Footprint method's impact categories: never set it beside the EU's own (territorial) emissions. The report doesn't say Figure 3-4's shares come from the same calculation, though both cite FIGARO (2024 edition) and EXIOBASE 3.8.2: the note says so.
- **F65.** Recycled aggregate replaces natural aggregate, not cement. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The EEA finds construction and demolition waste recovery largely based on backfilling and low-grade uses, such as recycled aggregates in road sub-bases. EEA Briefing 14/2019. Confirmed, through search results. ## Way forward

- **F26.** New buildings over 1,000 m² must have their whole-life carbon calculated and disclosed from January 1, 2028, and all new buildings from January 1, 2030. EPBD recast, Commission page ("What buildings fall under these rules?"); Directive (EU) 2024/1275, Article 7(2). Quote: "calculated and disclosed in the energy performance certificate." Confirmed. Article numbers come from secondary summaries.
- **F27.** By January 1, 2027, each member state must publish a roadmap for limit values on the whole-life carbon of all new buildings, with targets from 2030. EPBD recast, Commission page; Article 7(5). Confirmed. The Commission adopted the calculation framework on December 16, 2025.
- **F67.** No published source was found saying that keeping a building standing lowers the rate against demolishing and recycling it, or that the rate penalizes reuse. The nearest say the rate leaves such strategies out: it "does not explicitly include the effect of strategies" such as sharing, lifetime extension and remanufacturing, which prevent waste (Christis and Vercalsteren 2021, section 3.1.2, p. 25; through an automated fetch), and the EEA's briefing on the Netherlands says the indicator "does not take into account more high-value circular strategies" (through an automated fetch). Published work reads longer lifetimes as raising the rate, through less new material: the ETC lists "increasing the usable lifetime of materials currently in stock" as a way to improve it (ETC CE 2023/6, key message 2.3), and Christis and Vercalsteren list longer product lifespans the same way (section 4.3, p. 38). The ETC also says more intensive use of buildings, and construction and demolition waste, could alter the rate significantly, without saying which way (section 4.4, p. 36). Those compare keeping things with building new; none compares keeping with tearing down and rebuilding. The ZIN beat stays our reading of Eurostat's method (F57), and holds only against demolishing and rebuilding.
- **F28.** For homes in the G7 and China, material efficiency could cut material-cycle emissions by at least 80% in 2050. IRP 2020, foreword and summary for policymakers. Quote: "reduced by at least 80 per cent in 2050." Confirmed. Material cycle only: counting operational energy, the IRP's page gives 40% for the G7. By the report's factsheet, more intensive use of homes saves the most (up to 70% in the G7 in 2050), better recycling of construction materials 14 to 18%, and designs using less material 8 to 10%. The 70% is for more intensive use alone, not longer lifetimes. Confirmed, through search results; on November 21, a copy of the factsheet's text, seen through search results, gave all three for the G7 in 2050.
- **F29.** Austria pairs an 18% rate target for 2030 with caps on use: 14 tonnes per person by 2030 and a 7-tonne footprint by 2050. Austria's strategy, the ministry's page. Confirmed, through search results. By a press release on its second progress report, Austria's material use fell 16% from 2021 to 2024, to 15.7 tonnes per person: seen in summary.
- **F52.** Germany's National Circular Economy Strategy, adopted December 2024, aims at "doubling the percentage of secondary raw materials in the total quantity of all raw materials used by 2030" (the circular material use rate), under the guiding principle of "significantly reducing the quantity of primary raw materials used for consumption and investment in Germany" by 2045, measured as raw material consumption (RMC), with the International Resource Panel's 6 to 8 tonnes per person by 2050 as guidance. The adopted summary gives no number for Germany; a June 2024 draft gave 8 tonnes per person by 2045 (Clean Energy Wire; seen in summary). RMC is a footprint, not the material consumed (DMC) the page uses, so never set the two side by side. BMUV, English summary of the strategy. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. Backs 20a's default line.
- **F30.** Halving primary abiotic material use by 2030 was the Dutch goal; PBL's 2025 assessment rated it out of reach. PBL 2025. Seen in summary.
- **F31.** A differently scoped 15% cut by 2035 has since replaced the Dutch halving goal. NL Times, undated. Seen in summary; one news report.
- **F32.** Japan has steered by a near-identical ratio since 2003: about 10% in fiscal 2000, 16% in fiscal 2015 and 18% targeted for fiscal 2025. Japan's Fundamental Plan. Seen in summary; its path after 2015 is unverified.
- **F33.** The Circular Economy Act was still unpublished on November 16, 2026. The Commission's initiative page plans adoption for the fourth quarter of 2026. After slipping from November 11 to November 18, it is now on the Commission's agenda for December 2, by trade press on November 13 (packaging journal; EUWID); a newsletter of early November says no date is confirmed (Publyon), and Agence Europe (F42) says "expected in 2027." Commission initiative page, through search results; the rest seen in summary. Recheck before publishing.

## What the EU already measures

- **F77.** The 8th Environment Action Programme lists, among the enabling conditions for its objectives, "significantly decreasing the Union's material and consumption footprints to bring them into planetary boundaries as soon as possible, including through the introduction of Union 2030 reduction targets, as appropriate" (Article 3(s)), and closing gaps in indicators on the Union's production and consumption footprints (Article 3(z)). It sets no date for the targets and names no number. They are enabling conditions, which the attainment of the priority objectives "shall require" from "the Commission, Member States, regional and local authorities and stakeholders, as appropriate" (Article 3, opening words): so the programme names the targets as a condition for its goals, not an order to set them. Decision (EU) 2022/591, Article 3, opening words, (s) and (z). Confirmed against the EUR-Lex text, read by Milcah on November 22. The EEA calls it "a non-binding, EU-wide target" (Europe's environment 2025, briefing on waste generation and material consumption; through search results).
- **F78.** The Commission's monitoring targets for both footprints carry no number. Raw material consumption: "significantly decrease the EU's material footprint, by reducing the amount of raw material needed to produce the products consumed in the EU" (headline indicator 5). Consumption footprint: "significantly decrease the EU's consumption footprint, i.e. the environmental impact of consumption" (indicator 24). Most targets around them have one: net greenhouse gas emissions at least 55% below 1990 by 2030 (1), energy consumption at least 13% below 2020 (12), 25% of farmland organic (16), 30% of land and sea protected (9). The rate's target is to "double the ratio of circular material use by 2030 compared to 2020" (14). So in the EU's own monitoring table, the rate is the circular economy target with a number and the footprints have none. COM(2022) 357, table of headline indicators and targets. Confirmed against the table, read by Milcah as images on November 22. The EEA's 2023 monitoring report judged it "very unlikely" that the consumption footprint target will be met by 2030 (chapter 8; through search results).
- **F79.** The consumption footprint counts what the EU's consumption costs the environment, not its weight: a JRC indicator built from life cycle assessment of a basket of representative products, scaled to all EU consumption, scored on the Commission's Environmental Footprint method in 16 impact categories, climate change among them. It is an 8th EAP headline indicator; in the EEA's 2024 edition, climate change was the largest contributor to the single score (23%), and the EU exceeded its fair share of planetary boundaries in five categories in 2022, climate change among them. EEA, 8th EAP headline indicator 25, "Consumption footprint (based on life cycle assessment)," 2023, 2024 and 2025 editions; JRC134679. Through search results. Since May 2023, the Commission's circular economy monitoring framework also includes it, with greenhouse gas emissions from production activities, under a new heading on global sustainability and resilience (COM(2023) 306; SWD(2023) 306, sections 5.10 a and b; through search results). This is the footprint the 2020 plan named beside the doubling (F37).
- **F80.** The European Parliament asked the Commission to propose binding 2030 targets to significantly reduce the EU's material and consumption footprints and bring them within planetary boundaries by 2050, by 574 votes to 22, with 95 abstentions. Resolution of February 10, 2021 on the new Circular Economy Action Plan, 2020/2077(INI), rapporteur Jan Huitema. Through search results (Legislative Observatory summary; Parliament press release). Non-binding.
- **F81.** The Council acknowledged that the EU's material and consumption footprints are "still far beyond what is sustainable" and progress on circular material use too slow, recalled the 8th EAP's aim with "EU reduction targets, as appropriate" (paragraph 25), and invited the Commission to assess an EU long-term objective for sustainable resource use and science-based targets for material and consumption footprints within planetary boundaries, translated to national level (paragraph 28(b) and (c)). Paragraph 28(c) reads "the setting of ambitious and economically feasible science-based targets to keep material and consumption footprints within the planetary boundaries and their translation to the national level"; 28(d) asks whether existing law could be revised or "a new legislative act is needed." Council conclusions on the 8th EAP mid-term review, document 11326/24, approved June 17, 2024. Confirmed: paragraphs 25 and 28, read by Milcah on November 22. The Council's later conclusions, "Europe's Environment 2030" (document 16856/25, December 16, 2025), welcome the Circular Economy Act and acknowledge the need for "prevention of material extraction" (paragraph 57), but mention no footprint, target or rate. Through an automated fetch.
- **F82.** The Commission's mid-term review of the 8th EAP reports the material footprint at 14.8 tonnes per person in 2022, up 6% over the decade, and the consumption footprint up 4% from 2010 to 2021, beyond planetary boundaries, but does not commit to assessing or proposing footprint targets. COM(2024) 123, sections 2.3 and 2.6 (in Council document 7702/24). Through an automated fetch; the staff working document, SWD(2024) 60, not read.
- **F83.** The EEA says "Circular policies should become more binding and target-oriented, extending beyond waste to possibly include targets on resource use or material footprint," and that additional action is needed on the demand side, "potentially leveraging the concept of sufficiency." Its overall key messages say that, given "the impossibility of 100% circularity, it is crucial to prioritise the reduction of resource use," and that making the most of existing products "requires significantly more intensity of use per product and much longer product lifetimes." EEA, "Accelerating the circular economy in Europe: state and outlook 2024," March 21, 2024, executive summary, p. 7. Confirmed, read by Milcah on November 22. IEEP's brief on the Circular Economy Act argues a target or targets to reduce the EU's material footprint "would send a crucial signal," without proposing a level or year, and names Austria, Belgium, Finland and the Netherlands as having national material consumption reduction targets (citing OVAM 2023). Watkins, IEEP, June 2025. Through an automated fetch. Responses to the Act's call for evidence (August 1 to November 6, 2025) included calls for binding material footprint or resource use targets (WWF Germany, Generation Climate Europe, Circul'R) and, from the waste industry's FEAD, a binding 25% rate by 2030. Seen in summary.
- **F84.** The EU's waste recycling targets are set by weight: at least 70% "by weight" of non-hazardous construction and demolition waste prepared for reuse, recycled or otherwise recovered by 2020, backfilling included; and municipal waste prepared for reuse and recycled to at least 55%, 60% and 65% "by weight" by 2025, 2030 and 2035, the weight counted as it enters recycling. Directive 2008/98/EC as amended, Article 11(2)(b) to (e) and Article 11a. Confirmed, through the legislation.gov.uk copy of Article 11 and the Commission's Waste Framework Directive page (through search results). The construction target counts backfilling, which the rate leaves out (F57).
- **F85.** Since January 1, 2026, importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen must buy certificates for the greenhouse gases embedded in what they import, priced on the EU Emissions Trading System. Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, Regulation (EU) 2023/956; Commission (DG TAXUD) CBAM page. Through search results. Certificates for 2026's imports are bought from February 2027 (S&P Global, January 7, 2026; seen in summary).
- **F86.** The construction products declaration of performance and conformity must cover at least the product's life-cycle "climate change effects" (total, fossil fuels, biogenic, and land use and land use change; Annex II points (a) to (d)) from January 8, 2026, ozone depletion and the next characteristics (points (e) to (m)) from January 9, 2030, and points (n) to (s) from January 9, 2032. Under recital 110, a product family's requirements apply 12 months after its harmonized technical specification enters into force, so declarations phase in product family by product family. Regulation (EU) 2024/3110, Article 15(3) and Annex II. Through an automated fetch of the Official Journal text (EOTA copy); Article 15 confirmed against the text read by Milcah on November 22. A declaration of performance and conformity exists only for products under a harmonized technical specification of the new regulation, and the old regulation's standards run in parallel until January 8, 2040, being replaced product family by product family (Commission, "CPR 2024 revision" page; through search results). One tracker found no new harmonized standard published as of May 2026 (ecoverity; seen in summary). So the climate declarations are law but have barely started, if at all: on the page, "will have to declare," not "are declaring."
- **F87.** Ecodesign requirements can cover a product's carbon and environmental footprint, among about 20 product parameters, and the first working plan puts iron and steel (indicative adoption 2026) and aluminium (2027) first among intermediate products. Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, Annex I; Ecodesign for Sustainable Products and Energy Labelling Working Plan 2025 to 2030, COM(2025) 187, April 16, 2025. Seen in summary (EUR-Lex summary; Sidley, April 29, 2025).
- **F88.** Electric vehicle batteries, light transport batteries and rechargeable industrial batteries over 2 kWh must declare their carbon footprint, then carry a performance class and finally stay under a maximum, but each step applies only after the Commission adopts the calculation method; the method for electric vehicle batteries, due by February 18, 2024 and published in draft in April 2024, had not been adopted by October 2026. Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, Article 7. Seen in summary (Batterypass, "Deadlines of the Batteries Regulation," October 2026; Traceable Digital, September 2026). Keep off the page as a working rule.
- **F89.** The Industrial Accelerator Act, proposed March 4, 2026, would set low-carbon and/or made-in-EU requirements for steel, cement and aluminium in public procurement and support schemes: by the EEB's reading, 25% low-carbon for steel and aluminium and 5% for concrete and mortar; the voluntary low-carbon steel label was left to ecodesign rules. Commission newsroom, "Industrial Accelerator Act" (through search results); EEB, March 2026, and Eurometal (seen in summary). A proposal, not law.
- **F90.** Published work on weight-based measures: mass-based indicators such as recycling rates "fail to cover the environmental perspective," and an impact-based indicator of environmental value retained is proposed to complement them (Haupt and Hellweg 2019, abstract). The authors write that "monitoring both material consumption and environmental impacts as well as defining targets are key," that most strategies "include only mass-based targets," with "limited quantitative targets for the environmental performance" (section 1), and that the indicator should consider climate impact, supplemented with as many other impact categories as needed (section 2.1). Their examples show circular options aren't automatically better: remanufacturing an iron motor block beats a new one only if the alternative is the same iron motor, not a lighter aluminium one that saves fuel in use (sections 2.2.3 and 3). Confirmed, read in full from the ETH Research Collection copy, November 22. Recirculating more material isn't necessarily more sustainable (Blum, Haupt and Bening 2020, a perspective; through search results). Official recycling rates can overstate what actually returns to use (Haupt, Vadenbo and Hellweg 2017; seen in summary). Ireland's EPA finds the rate "mass based and therefore does not directly consider other environmental pressures such as greenhouse gas emissions" (McCarthy et al., EPA Research Report 458, 2024; through an automated fetch of its one-page summary). With F20, the case that weight isn't impact is published, not ours.

## Countries

- **F99.** Ireland has one of the lowest circularity rates in the EU. In env_ac_cur as downloaded (last updated November 24, 2025), it was the second lowest of the 27 in 2022 and 2023, after Romania, and joint second lowest with Finland in 2024, a year Eurostat imputed for 26 countries (`headline_numbers_2022.csv`, `ireland_rank_from_lowest_*`). - **F69.** Estonia's swings are rock from its oil shale mines. In env_wastrt, Estonia's recycled "other mineral wastes" (W12B, part of the page's soil and rock) went from 3.2 Mt in 2010 to 8.8 Mt in 2012 and 3.7 Mt in 2014, against all recycling of 6.0, 12.1 and 6.6 Mt; in env_wasgen, mining and quarrying generated 6.4, 9.3 and 7.9 Mt of W12B in those years, nearly all of it (our calculation, November 22). The Estonian Environment Agency says recovery rose sharply in 2012, "mainly waste rock" (põhiliselt aheraine), the rock separated from oil shale, used mostly as crushed aggregate under R5; more than 13.5 Mt of it built a recreation center with a motocross track in Mäetaguse in 2011 to 2013, and waste rock sent to landfill rose again to about 2 Mt in 2013. Keskkonnaagentuur 2015, pp. 19 to 21. Through an automated fetch of the Estonian text, translated by the tool. Oil shale is Estonia's largest source of industrial waste (OECD Environmental Performance Reviews: Estonia 2017; through search results). Since 2020, W12B is also generated by energy (2.5 Mt in 2020, 6.5 Mt in 2022), likely oil shale ash (not checked); the EEA's Estonia briefing says most recycled waste is oil shale ash, which fits recent years, not 2010 to 2014. The 13.5 Mt hill counts as recycling though it replaced little or no new material: a case of recycling's second hope failing.
- **F70.** The Netherlands' export share: Statistics Netherlands says the material input per person destined for exports is higher there than elsewhere in the EU, as it imports goods that are slightly processed and re-exported, and that its consumption per person (DMC) was among the EU's lowest from 2000 to 2018. CBS, February 21, 2020. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. It doesn't link either to the rate. The EEA's Netherlands briefing credits the rate mainly to construction and demolition waste used under new roads (through an automated fetch). ## The terrazzo

- **F91.** Roman floors from what was left over: opus signinum is lime mortar with crushed pottery, tile or brick as its aggregate, used to line baths, cisterns and aqueducts and, in floors, for damp-proofing. Pliny describes it: broken pottery, "beaten to powder, and tempered with lime," becomes "more solid and durable than other substances" (Natural History, Book 35, in the chapter on works in pottery, 35.46 as Wikipedia cites it). The technique is documented first at Tell el-Burak (Lebanon) and spread through Phoenician North Africa to Sicily and Italy. Pliny's sentence confirmed through an automated fetch of the Bostock and Riley translation; the rest from Wikipedia ("Opus signinum") and Martínez Jiménez et al. 2024, abstract, through search results.
- **F92.** The scraps story is the trade's own: the National Terrazzo and Mosaic Association (NTMA) says terrazzo was created when Venetian mosaic workers reused marble remnants, building terraces around their living quarters with the off-size chips, and calls it one of the world's first green flooring systems. Popular sources date this to the 15th century; NTMA's own timeline dates the first Venetian terrazzo floors to the 16th century, by workers from Friuli, and gives a documented date: on February 9, 1583, Venice's Council of Ten granted Sgualdo Sabadin of Provesano and his fellow Friulian terazzeri a right to work (the grant's wording cut off in search results). No historian's source for the scraps origin was found. The page frames it as "as the trade tells it." Through search results.
- **F93.** The scholarly account: the form of terrazzo used today derives partly from the 18th-century pavimento alla veneziana and the cheaper seminato; terrazzo reached the United States in the late 1890s, was hand polished with the long-handled galera until then, and became popular in the 1920s (Johnson, "Terrazzo," in Jester, ed., Twentieth-Century Building Materials, 1995, page 203). Seen in summary, through Wikipedia's citation: the Getty's 2014 PDF couldn't be fetched. Not on the page; open it before citing. Goat's milk as the first sealer is NTMA's lore, undated; not on the page.
- **F94.** Italian workers brought it to the United States: the first Italian mosaic workers came around 1875, and by 1890 all mosaic workers in the US were Italians trained in Venice, from Friulian towns such as Sequals (NTMA). In 1926, 40 of NTMA's 60 firms were Italian-owned (NTMA). Through search results.
- **F95.** What made it common: European patents for metal divider strips date from 1893, to stop cracking (NTMA, "A Brief History of the Terrazzo Divider Strip"); the electric grinding machine, around 1919, spread terrazzo across the US, and in the early 1920s terrazzo overtook mosaic in popularity (NTMA). Today's aggregates can include pre- and post-consumer recycled glass, porcelain and mirror chips (NTMA). Through search results. Not on the page: the claims that the immigrants introduced Portland cement and that 18th-century Venetians sealed floors with linseed oil, for which no source was found.
