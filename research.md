## Context

- **F01.** About half of the materials extracted worldwide each year go into building up or renewing stocks: buildings, infrastructure and machinery. World, 2010. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F02.** Recycling supplies only 12% of the material flowing into those stocks. World, 2010. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Quote: "recycling still only contributes 12% of inflows to stocks." Confirmed.
- **F03.** Global material stocks grew 23-fold from 1900 to 2010, to 792 billion tonnes (plus or minus 5%). Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F04.** The authors' remedy is more service from existing stocks: use them more intensively, make them last longer and design them more efficiently. Krausmann et al. 2017, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F05.** The EU's circular material use rate rose from about 11.3% in 2015 to 11.7% in 2021. ECA 2023, paragraphs 25 and 93. Confirmed. Read in the French version, on Eurostat data as published in 2023.
- **F06.** Seven member states' rates fell over the same period. ECA 2023, paragraph 26. Confirmed. The report text names only Poland; news coverage lists Lithuania, Sweden, Romania, Denmark, Luxembourg, Finland and Poland.
- **F07.** The EU planned more than €10 billion for the circular economy in 2014 to 2020, and about three quarters of the €7.1 billion cohesion policy share was tied to EU waste law. ECA 2023, paragraphs IX, 55 and 59. Confirmed. Not "spent," and not 2016 to 2020 as some news coverage says.
- **F08.** Low circularity traces to energy use, 44% of processed materials, and to stocks that keep growing, with net additions of 17 Gt a year. World, 2005. The EU's circularity is low for similar reasons, despite its considerably higher end-of-life recycling rates. Haas et al. 2015, abstract. Seen in summary; the 17 Gt and the EU sentence confirmed against the abstract, through search results.
- **F09.** The EU28's input cycling rate was 9.6% in 2014, and the authors want mass indicators split by material to judge impact. Eurostat's Sankey descends from this framework. Mayer et al. 2019. Seen in summary.

## Main story

- **F10.** New material used is about eight times the recycled material used, so the rate follows material use more than recycling. EU-27, around 2021. EEA 2023, under "Main factors influencing the circular material use rate." Quote: "influenced more by trends in material consumption than by trends in recycling." Confirmed.
- **F11.** Recycling 70% of treated waste (40% in 2020), using 15% less material than in 2020 and a third less fossil fuel (34% against the baseline) together lift the rate to about 22% by 2030, short of 23.4%. No lever reaches the target alone, and the briefing gives no rate for each. EEA 2023, under "Prospects for the EU reaching its target" and in the key messages. Confirmed.
- **F12.** Doubling from 11.7% in 2020 to 23.4% in 2030 means the rate climbing by 1.3% of material used each year, against 0.1% a year over "the past decade," which the briefing doesn't define; it gives 0.4% a year for 2004 to 2010. The EEA's 23.4% doubles the 2020 rate; the page's 24% is the Clean Industrial Deal's (F34). EEA 2023, same section. Confirmed.
- **F13.** The world economy was more circular in 1900 than in 2015: the share of material inputs that cycled, biomass included, fell from 43% (41 to 51%) to 27% (25 to 30%), while non-circular inputs grew 16-fold. Biomass's ecological cycling made up 91% of cycling in 1900 and 76% in 2015. Haas et al. 2020, abstract. Confirmed.
- **F14.** The authors rank absolute cuts in non-circular flows above maximizing recycling rates, one of four challenges they name with curbing stock growth, criteria for ecological cycling and decarbonizing energy. Haas et al. 2020, abstract. Quote: "prioritizing absolute reductions of non-circular flows over maximizing (re)cycling rates." Confirmed.
- **F15.** A review of 835 studies found few lasting cases of growth with falling resource use; the authors call for sufficiency and enforced absolute reduction targets. Haberl et al. 2020. Seen in summary.
- **F16.** A recycled tonne doesn't automatically cancel a new one; the paper calls this circular economy rebound. Zink and Geyer 2017. Seen in summary.
- **F34.** The EU aims for 24% of its materials to be circular by 2030. Clean Industrial Deal, Commission page, under "Circularity and access to materials." Quote: "The aim is to have 24% of materials circular by 2030." Confirmed. Worded as an aim; the same section commits to adopting a circular economy act in 2026.
- **F49.** The EEA's indicator page gives the doubling as 22.4% by 2030 and the rate as 12.2% in 2024. The repo's 2020 rate is 11.2%, so 22.4% doubles the revised 2020 figure, where the EEA's 2023 briefing doubled 11.7% to 23.4% (F12) and the Deal says 24% (F34). EEA indicator, "Circular material use rate in Europe." Confirmed, through search results.
- **F60.** The EEA's 2025 assessment says the rate rose from 10.7% in 2010 to 12.2% in 2024, the rise to 2022 "largely driven by increased waste recycling" and the rise from 2022 to 2024 by lower domestic material consumption, "mainly due to decreased use of metals." By weight that doesn't hold in Eurostat's DMC, where the 2022 to 2024 fall was 56.9% fossil fuels and 42.9% minerals (`headline_numbers_2022.csv`): don't cite this attribution. It names reducing material consumption, through reuse and longer product lifetimes, beside boosting recycling as ways to reach the target, and judges the target "very unlikely." EEA, Monitoring report on progress towards the 8th EAP objectives 2025, December 2025, section 6.3. Confirmed.

## Recycling replacing new material

The page assumes each extra recycled tonne replaces half a new one, a midpoint between replacing none and replacing a whole tonne (brief, The piece). These findings back the two bounds and the limits of replacement.

- **F54.** ETC CE 2025/3 models enhanced recycling with 1:1 substitution: "we assume a 1:1 substitution rate in which 1 kg of waste sent to recycling replaces 1 kg of primary materials"; footnote 34: "this is likely an overestimation"; and "this scenario should be considered as an upper limit of the potential of enhanced recycling." Primary production falls through reduced demand, so consumption isn't held stable. ETC CE 2025/3, section 4.3 (results in 5.3). Confirmed, through an automated fetch.
- **F55.** Eurostat reads a higher rate as more secondary materials substituting for primary raw materials, avoiding the impacts of extracting them. Eurostat, metadata for the circularity rate (env_ac_cur), section 3.1. Confirmed, through search results. The indicator's own framing assumes substitution; it doesn't measure it.
- **F56.** Displacement is set by markets, not physics, and is not guaranteed. For US aluminum, 100% displacement is unlikely right after a lasting rise in recycling and even less likely in the long term; zero and even negative displacement are possible, and the estimates are sensitive to price elasticities. Zink, Geyer and Startz 2018, abstract. Confirmed, through search results. One material, one country: no economy-wide rate. The authors' 2016 paper notes that assessments have tended to assume one-to-one displacement because no consensus method exists. No sourced middle-ground rate (such as 50%) has been found for the EU's materials.
- **F57.** In 2022, 40.8% of EU waste was recycled and 30.2% landfilled, and 64% of all waste generated was major mineral waste, from mining, quarrying, construction and demolition. Eurostat, Statistics Explained, "Waste statistics." Confirmed, through search results (the 64% source split from the European Parliament's summary of the same data). The 40.8% matches the rate's 41% of treated waste recycled. The rate's recycled waste is recycling alone, R2 to R11, energy recovery and backfilling excluded, from env_wastrt, net of waste traded for recycling (env_ac_cur metadata, sections 3.1, 3.4 and 18.1; confirmed, through an automated fetch); rebuilt that way in the repo, 41.1% of treated waste was recycled in 2022. The waste not yet recycled is mostly minerals, checked November 20 in the repo (`waste_by_material_2022.csv`, `headline_numbers_2022.csv`): 86.5% by material (env_wassd), and 75.8% major mineral waste by category, about 82% with ash and mineral residues (env_wastrt).
- **F59.** In a model of the copper market, permanent increases in recycling displace about 0.5 kt of mine production per kt of extra scrap supply on average, short-lived recycling policies can raise mine production, and displacement is lower for large changes in scrap supply. Ryter et al. 2022, abstract. Confirmed, through search results. One metal: scale only, never the basis for the page's midpoint.

## Holding recycling fixed

Still the rule for cuts: when the reader or a scenario stops using new material, waste and recycling stay at 2022's tonnes.

- **F46.** The EEA's scenarios for the doubling target cut material consumption while keeping circular use constant, and balance the mass through less stock accumulation and fewer emissions, not less waste (section 4.2, Table 2). The 70% recycling scenario holds consumption, waste traded and waste generated stable (section 4.3.1), so in it recycled tonnes add to new ones; the page's midpoint doesn't follow that part (F54). The EEA's chart of the scenarios calls them a first indicative estimate, without a full mass-balancing exercise. The report also says that using less fossil fuel and biomass for energy, and preventing food waste, lower the volumes available for recycling (section 2.3.2, p. 15); that biomass and fossil fuels largely become emissions, while metals and minerals go into stock and become available for recycling later (section 2.3.3, pp. 17 to 18); and that long-lasting products reach recycling after a delay (section 4.1). Its combined 2050 scenario (90% recycling, 45% less material and 83% less fossil fuel) reaches 39% (key message 4.4, p. 25). ETC CE Report 2023/6. Quote: "while keeping U constant." Confirmed, through an automated fetch of the PDF's first 100,000 of 169,506 characters, to section 4.3.1.
- **F47.** The EEA's country briefings record rates running high when consumption fell but recycling didn't. In Belgium, recycling fell less than material consumption, so the rate was exceptionally high in those years; in Estonia, the rate peaked in 2012, when consumption was lower but recycling about the same. EEA, Europe's environment 2025, country briefings on the circular material use rate. Confirmed, through search results.
- **F48.** Dynamic stock-flow models trace how material inputs build in-use stocks and come back as end-of-life waste over their lifetimes: MISO, an inflow-driven extension of economy-wide material flow accounting (Wiedenhofer et al. 2019), and MISO2, a country-level version covering 14 processes from extraction to recycling and waste management, and 13 end-uses of stocks (Wiedenhofer et al. 2024). The route to modeling how waste follows material use over time; not used on the page. Seen in summary.
- **F58.** Growing stocks keep material loops open: when inflows to stocks are a multiple of outflows, closing loops is hard even with better end-of-life recycling, and if net additions keep declining, stocks in the industrial world may saturate, which "would increase the potential for closed loops and absolute reductions in primary material extraction." The authors expect 35% of the 2010 stock to be discarded by 2030. World. Krausmann et al. 2017, discussion. Confirmed, through an automated fetch of the full text. For the United States, "Stabilized stocks also result in stable EoL outflows after 2050, whereas ongoing stock growth makes outflows grow until 2080." Streeck et al. 2021, abstract. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. Neither models waste falling with a cut in use: that the link between use and waste tightens as stocks stop growing is our reading.

## The economic case

- **F37.** The doubling goal dates from the 2020 Circular Economy Action Plan, which set it in the same sentence as cutting the consumption footprint, under the subtitle "For a cleaner and more competitive Europe." The plan says EU manufacturing firms spend on average about 40% on materials, and cites a study estimating an extra 0.5% of GDP and around 700,000 jobs by 2030 (Cambridge Econometrics, Trinomics and ICF 2018; not used, see the brief's Flags). COM(2020) 98, Introduction. Quote: "strive to reduce its consumption footprint and double its circular material use rate in the coming decade." Confirmed, through an automated fetch of a mirror copy; cite EUR-Lex on the page.
- **F38.** The Clean Industrial Deal's communication makes the rate a KPI: "Increase circular material use rate from 11.8% today to 24% by 2030," with footnote 28 citing the 2020 plan (F37) and footnote 29 saying progress is reported in the Annual Single Market and Competitiveness Report. Its flagship table lists the Circular Economy Act for Q4 2026. It gives circularity's reasons as maximizing limited resources, reducing dependencies, resilience, lower production costs and lower CO2, and projects the remanufacturing market growing from €31 billion to €100 billion by 2030, creating 500,000 jobs (not used). It never mentions material consumption, sufficiency or using less: "footprint" appears once, for labels showing consumers the carbon footprint of products (section 3), "consumption" only for energy, and "sufficient" only in its everyday sense. COM(2025) 85, sections 1, 3 and 5 and the flagship actions table. Confirmed: searched by hand in the EUR-Lex PDF by Milcah and by script on November 21.
- **F39.** The EU is almost self-sufficient in non-metallic minerals and biomass and highly dependent on imported metal ores and fossil fuels. Import dependency, decade averages: minerals 3%, biomass 11%, metal ores 51%, fossil fuels 70% (env_ac_mid; data from June 2026). Eurostat, "Physical imports and exports," Table 1. Quote: "The EU economy is almost self-sufficient in the supply of non-metallic minerals (notably construction materials) and biomass." Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The page's 2022 values come from the repo (brief, "Numbers behind it").
- **F40.** The Critical Raw Materials Act sets 2030 benchmarks for 17 strategic raw materials (Annex I): EU capacity able to mine at least 10% of the EU's yearly consumption of them, process at least 40% and recycle at least 25%, and no more than 65% of yearly consumption of any one of them from a single third country. They are benchmarks for capacity that the EU should "approach or reach," not shares of actual supply. It also asks the Commission and member states to "moderate the expected increase" in the EU's consumption of critical raw materials through technological progress and resource efficiency. In force since May 23, 2024. Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, Article 5(1) and (2), Annex I. Confirmed.
- **F41.** Of the 26 critical raw materials the EU needs for the energy transition, 10 are not recycled at all (lithium, gallium and silicon metal among them) and 7 have end-of-life recycling input rates of 1 to 5%. The ECA also finds that the potential to cut the EU's need for primary critical raw materials through circularity, substitution and resource efficiency is "not fully used." ECA Special Report 04/2026, adopted December 10, 2025, paragraphs 18, 19 and 83 and Figure 19. Confirmed.
- **F42.** Commissioner Roswall's list for the Circular Economy Act on November 13, 2026: one extended producer responsibility registration through a single representative, harmonized and faster end-of-waste criteria, more demand for recycled materials and circular products, stronger e-waste collection to recover critical raw materials, and transregional circularity hubs. No material use target or 24% rate mentioned. The article says the Act is "expected in 2027" without saying whether that means the proposal. Agence Europe, November 13, 2026. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The Regulatory Scrutiny Board's main criticism, by Roswall's account, was that the demand side wasn't addressed enough (EUWID, October 2026; seen in summary).
- **F43.** A European Parliament briefing on the Act records 4.3 million people working in the EU's circular economy (2021), a material footprint of 14.1 tonnes per person (2024) far beyond sustainable levels, the Council's call for EU reduction targets, and the EEA's view that absolute cuts in resource use are needed. It repeats the 0.5% of GDP and 700,000 jobs without a source. EPRS briefing PE 782.628, Camille Siefridt, January 2026. Confirmed, through an automated fetch.
- **F44.** EU gas demand fell 18% between August 2022 and December 2023 (about 101 bcm), against a voluntary 15% target under Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1369. Commission review COM(2024) 88, in Council document 6566/24. Seen in summary.
- **F45.** The EEA's material footprint indicator credits the fall in fossil fuel use to climate policies and recent energy developments, such as high prices after Russia's invasion of Ukraine; metal use fell 16% in 2023. EEA, 8th EAP monitoring report 2025, indicator 05. Seen in summary. The printed monitoring report (December 2025, section 3.1) puts the footprint's 6.2% fall in 2023 down "mainly" to lower use of fossil fuels and metals, "resulting from the geopolitical response to the war in Ukraine," and names no longer-running climate policies; confirmed. Backs panel 11's judgment only in part: open the indicator itself before the card is final.

## Weight versus climate

- **F17.** Non-metallic minerals are 52.3% of EU material use by weight but 11.4% of the climate impact of producing materials. EU-27, 2022, extraction to factory gate. ETC CE 2025/3, Figure 3-4 (about page 12), red climate series. Confirmed against the figure. All four groups' values are in `build_data.py` (WEIGHT_VS_CLIMATE); metal ores are 16.3% of climate impact, and their 30.2% is particulate matter.
- **F18.** Biomass is 22.7% of the weight and 99.5% of land-use biodiversity loss. ETC CE 2025/3, Figure 3-4; the text rounds it to 99%, on IRP GRO 2024 data. Confirmed.
- **F19.** The impacts cover making each material ready to use, up to the factory gate; burning fuel and later uses aren't allocated to the materials. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4 and the box in section 4.4. Confirmed. The chart needs a label saying so, or the fossil fuel share looks too small.
- **F20.** A higher circularity rate doesn't necessarily mean lower environmental impact. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4 and key messages (page 2). Quote: "does not necessarily lead to lower environmental impacts." Confirmed.
- **F21.** Making ready-to-use materials accounts for around 80% of the EU's total environmental impacts, seen as a footprint; the rest comes from the rest of the economy and households' direct impacts. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4. Confirmed. Measured as the environmental footprint score, not climate alone.
- **F22.** An older EEA figure gave non-metallic minerals 6% of the environmental footprint of ready-to-use materials. EU-27, 2019. EEA 2023, Figure 4. Confirmed. A composite score on a different method: never set it beside the 11.4%.
- **F23.** Impact per kilogram spans orders of magnitude, and bulk materials rank low. van der Voet et al. 2004. Seen in summary.
- **F24.** Biomass drives more than 90% of land-related biodiversity loss and water stress. IRP Global Resources Outlook 2024. Seen in summary. Not the IRP's older line that all resource extraction and processing cause over 90%.
- **F25.** Producing materials caused 23% of global emissions in 2015, about two-fifths of it for construction. Hertwich 2021. Seen in summary. The IRP 2020 report page gives the same 23% for 2015, up from 15% in 1995.
- **F53.** The climate change impact of the raw materials used in the EU-27 was 2.89E+12 kg CO2-eq in 2022 (3.09E+12 in 2010, 2.74E+12 in 2021), so 2.89 billion tonnes, the total behind the game's carbon tonnes. A cradle-to-gate footprint of EU final demand, on the Environmental Footprint method's impact categories: never set it beside the EU's own (territorial) emissions. ETC CE 2025/3, section 3.4, Table 3-1, "Historical trend of environmental impacts of raw materials used in the EU-27." Confirmed, through an automated fetch. The report doesn't say Figure 3-4's shares come from the same calculation, though both cite FIGARO (2024 edition) and EXIOBASE 3.8.2: the note says so.

## Way forward

- **F26.** New buildings over 1,000 m² must have their whole-life carbon calculated and disclosed from January 1, 2028, and all new buildings from January 1, 2030. EPBD recast, Commission page ("What buildings fall under these rules?"); Directive (EU) 2024/1275, Article 7(2). Quote: "calculated and disclosed in the energy performance certificate." Confirmed. Article numbers come from secondary summaries.
- **F27.** By January 1, 2027, each member state must publish a roadmap for limit values on the whole-life carbon of all new buildings, with targets from 2030. EPBD recast, Commission page; Article 7(5). Confirmed. The Commission adopted the calculation framework on December 16, 2025.
- **F28.** For homes in the G7 and China, material efficiency could cut material-cycle emissions by at least 80% in 2050. IRP 2020, foreword and summary for policymakers. Quote: "reduced by at least 80 per cent in 2050." Confirmed. Material cycle only: counting operational energy, the IRP's page gives 40% for the G7. By the report's factsheet, more intensive use of homes saves the most (up to 70% in the G7 in 2050), better recycling of construction materials 14 to 18%, and designs using less material 8 to 10%. The 70% is for more intensive use alone, not longer lifetimes. Confirmed, through search results.
- **F29.** Austria pairs an 18% rate target for 2030 with caps on use: 14 tonnes per person by 2030 and a 7-tonne footprint by 2050. Austria's strategy, the ministry's page. Confirmed, through search results. By a press release on its second progress report, Austria's material use fell 16% from 2021 to 2024, to 15.7 tonnes per person: seen in summary.
- **F52.** Germany's National Circular Economy Strategy, adopted December 2024, aims at "doubling the percentage of secondary raw materials in the total quantity of all raw materials used by 2030" (the circular material use rate), under the guiding principle of "significantly reducing the quantity of primary raw materials used for consumption and investment in Germany" by 2045, measured as raw material consumption (RMC), with the International Resource Panel's 6 to 8 tonnes per person by 2050 as guidance. The adopted summary gives no number for Germany; a June 2024 draft gave 8 tonnes per person by 2045 (Clean Energy Wire; seen in summary). RMC is a footprint, not the material consumed (DMC) the page uses, so never set the two side by side. BMUV, English summary of the strategy. Confirmed, through an automated fetch. Backs 20a's default line.
- **F30.** Halving primary abiotic material use by 2030 was the Dutch goal; PBL's 2025 assessment rated it out of reach. PBL 2025. Seen in summary.
- **F31.** A differently scoped 15% cut by 2035 has since replaced the Dutch halving goal. NL Times, undated. Seen in summary; one news report.
- **F32.** Japan has steered by a near-identical ratio since 2003: about 10% in fiscal 2000, 16% in fiscal 2015 and 18% targeted for fiscal 2025. Japan's Fundamental Plan. Seen in summary; its path after 2015 is unverified.
- **F33.** The Circular Economy Act was still unpublished on November 16, 2026. The Commission's initiative page plans adoption for the fourth quarter of 2026. After slipping from November 11 to November 18, it is now on the Commission's agenda for December 2, by trade press on November 13 (packaging journal; EUWID); a newsletter of early November says no date is confirmed (Publyon), and Agence Europe (F42) says "expected in 2027." Commission initiative page, through search results; the rest seen in summary. Recheck before publishing.

## Examples

- **F35.** ZIN's per-m² figures imply about 116,300 m² (`examples.csv`, `implied_area`), close to the architects' built surface of 115,000 m² (51N4E) and above the 110,000 m² above ground that Equans gives (75,000 m² of offices, 14,000 of housing, 16,000 of hotel, leisure and shops). Completion dates differ by source: Dreso's role ran from September 2017 to April 2023, Zukunft Bau gives 2024, and Jaspers-Eyers lists it as completed in 2025. Cards give no year. Confirmed, through search results.
- **F36.** IKEA's Food is Precious kitchens weighed their waste on smart scales from two partners, Winnow and Leanpath (in 2017, Leanpath in the US and Winnow in Europe), so not every kitchen used Winnow. Ingka's 2022 release on the 54% cut credits AI through a partnership with Winnow. Ingka is the largest of 12 IKEA franchisees, with around 87% of IKEA's sales, and ran 411 IKEA stores on August 31, 2025. Waste Management World 2017; GreenMatters 2017; Ingka's 2022 release; Ingka's FAQ. Confirmed, through search results. The cut (November 19): the release, of September 20, 2022, footnotes its 54% as "As of July 2022" and the goal as "a combined reduction for all stores"; Ingka's FY22 report gives 57.8% "by the end of FY22 (from FY17)", the target ("Reduce production food waste by 50% by the end of 2021, compared with FY17") met by December 31, 2021, after its deadline was moved from August 31, 2020, and scoped to "the waste from our IKEA food service areas" before the food is sold, so not plate leftovers (pages 11, 14 and 64). The FY22 report states no unit; the FY24 report gives 60.5% since FY17 "(grams per cover)" and about 37.8 million portions saved, as quoted by Milcah from the report. FY22 runs September 1, 2021 to August 31, 2022. The page uses 57.8% for 2022 and keeps 60.5% for 2024 in the card. FY22 report confirmed, through automated fetches; release footnote through an automated fetch.
- **F50.** ZIN's upfront carbon by building element, against the benchmark: the benchmark totals 65,000 t of CO2 and ZIN about 43,500 t, 33% less. By eye, the whole saving sits in the foundations (about 6,000 t to almost none) and the load-bearing structure (about 35,500 t to about 20,000); finishes, facade and MEP are level. The totals over about 116,000 m² give the 561 and 377 kg per m², so the 377 covers the whole building. Split by element, not by material: the paper gives no split of the 171,000 t kept or of its carbon between concrete and steel (section 3.1 gives only the "structural mass" and "over 70,000 cubic meters of concrete"). Units are kg CO2 throughout, not CO2e; no LCA method or database is named (section 5). Leroy, Gomez and Nosiglia 2025, Figure 14, "Upfront carbon mitigation (Befimmo)." Confirmed, read from the figure by eye.
- **F51.** The 30,000 t of rubble from the WTC I/II towers was sorted and crushed into 3,500 t of A+ aggregates, which went into new concrete (the figure marks 30%), for 30,000 t of new C2C-certified concrete, trucked at most 15 km. The figure doesn't say what became of the rest of the rubble. Leroy, Gomez and Nosiglia 2025, Figure 5, "Recycled aggregates supply chain." Confirmed, read from the figure by eye. Card 12 and `rubble_to_aggregate` count all 30,000 t as made into aggregate (the abstract's wording): check section 3.3.
